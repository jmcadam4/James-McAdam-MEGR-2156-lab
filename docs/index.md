## Who I am as an engineer

I am a junior year mechanical engineering student as of fall 2026 and have enjoyed the pursuit of understanding mechanical systems and their designs for many years before I came to UNCC. I worked on bikes and cars a lot in middle school then found my way to robotics in high school. I knew for a long time that I wanted to be a mechanical engineer however the experience of working with a team on a tight schedule to get a working robot built and deployed really solidified that belief. A cohesive team can get a project done with a better result in a fraction of the time, and seeing it in practice made me start thinking about the logistics of getting a problem solved just as much as how to solve it. I don't see myself becoming an engineering manager where that intuition becomes really important but I still think it can help the team perform when everyone is thinking about things like that. These two practices, not alone but largely, make me excited to become a professional mechanical engineer soon.

## The Arc of This Course

This lab is not a survey of machines. It is a progression of design thinking.

Each technology I encounter this semester has its own design language. What works on a Prusa will not work on a Form 4. What works on a Form 4 will not work with a laser cutter. Learning to design *for the process* — to make decisions about geometry, tolerance, orientation, and material that reflect the specific capabilities and constraints of the machine — is the central skill this lab develops.

**Weeks 1–8: Fused Deposition Modeling (FDM)**
I begin with the Prusa Core One. FDM is the most accessible additive manufacturing technology, and the most forgiving — but designing well for it requires understanding layer adhesion, support structures, print orientation, and how thermoplastic behavior affects my final part. I will move from calibration to functional design, from single parts to assemblies, building toward a complete artifact that demonstrates everything I have learned about FDM design.

**Week 9: Laser Cutting**
The laser cutter introduces a fundamentally different design constraint: I are working in two dimensions to produce three-dimensional function. Joinery, press fits, and interlocking geometry become my primary tools. Parts that would be simple to print must be reconceived entirely for flat sheet material.

**Weeks 10–11: Stereolithography (SLA)**
The Form 4 resin printer produces parts with exceptional surface finish and dimensional accuracy — but it demands a different design approach. Support structures are critical. Resin behavior under load differs from thermoplastic. Tolerances that work in FDM must be recalibrated for SLA. Designing for the Form 4 means designing for precision.

**Weeks 12–15: Final Project**
The semester closes with an open-ended design challenge. I define the problem, select the technology, and see it through to a finished artifact. By this point I will have designed for three distinct processes. The final project asks I to apply that judgment — choosing not just how to design, but which process to design for and why.

Each stage builds on the last. Nothing is wasted.

## my Machines This Semester

**FDM — Prusa Core One**
Fused Deposition Modeling. Builds parts layer by layer from thermoplastic filament. Fast, accessible, and ideal for functional prototypes. my primary machine for the first half of the semester. Design considerations: layer orientation, overhang angles, support structures, wall thickness, and infill.

**Laser Cutter**
Precision cutting and engraving of flat sheet material. Design considerations: kerf width, joint clearance, material grain direction, and how two-dimensional geometry creates three-dimensional structure.

**SLA — Formlabs Form 4**
Stereolithography. Uses UV light to cure liquid resin into high-precision parts. Exceptional surface finish and dimensional accuracy. Design considerations: support placement, part orientation, resin shrinkage, and post-cure mechanical properties.

**SLS — Formlabs Fuse 1+** *(selected projects)*
Selective Laser Sintering. Produces strong, functional nylon parts without support structures. Design considerations: powder removal, wall thickness minimums, and how the absence of support structures changes my design freedom.

## How This Portfolio Works

Every lab entry answers three questions about my design process:

**What did the process require?**
Every additive manufacturing technology has design rules — constraints imposed by the machine, the material, and the process itself. Document what those rules were for this lab and how they shaped my design decisions.

**What did I design and why?**
What choices did I make? What geometry, orientation, tolerance, or material decision was specific to the process I were designing for? A good entry shows the reasoning behind the design, not just the finished part.

**What did I learn?**
What worked? What failed? What would I change if I printed it again? The most valuable entries in this portfolio will be the ones where something went wrong and I understood why.

That last question is the standard: could someone read my entry, understand my design decisions, and make better ones because of what I documented?
